Every coursework subject listed is open to questions. What are the OS must-knows?
  • Process vs thread: a process has its own memory; threads share their process's memory. Context-switching between threads is cheaper.
  • Deadlock conditions: mutual exclusion, hold-and-wait, no preemption, circular wait. Prevent it by breaking any one of them, e.g. always taking locks in a fixed order.

  • Paging and virtual memory: memory is split into pages; a page fault loads a missing page from disk. Replacement policies: FIFO, LRU, Optimal.
  • Mutex vs semaphore: a mutex lets one owner in; a semaphore is a counter that allows N.
Computer Networks must-knows
  • OSI and TCP/IP layers; where HTTP (application), TCP/UDP (transport), and IP (network) sit.
  • TCP vs UDP: TCP is reliable and ordered and opens with a 3-way handshake (SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK). UDP is faster with no guarantees, used for video and DNS.
  • What happens when you type a URL: DNS lookup, TCP connection, TLS handshake, HTTP request, server response, browser render.

"Why lag features? How did you compute them?"

Linear models and trees have no memory of time order, so lags turn the time series into a table. shift(1) uses only past values. A follow-up weakness: the rows mix all regions and models sorted by date, so shift(1) takes the previous row, which may be a different region or model. The correct way is df.groupby(["Region","Model"])["Estimated_Deliveries"].shift(1).

"Why Ridge and Lasso? Difference?"

Both are linear regression with a penalty to reduce overfitting. Ridge (L2) shrinks all coefficients and handles correlated features. Lasso (L1) can push coefficients to exactly 0, which selects features. All three scored about the same here, because the leaky feature dominated.

"Why TimeSeriesSplit and not KFold?"

KFold shuffles the data, so the model trains on the future and is tested on the past, which is leakage. TimeSeriesSplit always trains on earlier folds and tests on the next one.

"What is SARIMAX? What do (1,1,1)(1,1,1,12) mean?"

SARIMAX is Seasonal ARIMA with exogenous variables. The order (p,d,q) = (1,1,1) means 1 autoregressive lag, 1 differencing step, and 1 moving-average term. The seasonal order (P,D,Q,s) = (1,1,1,12) does the same with a yearly season of 12 months. You forecast monthly totals 6 months ahead. (The plot title is wrongly labelled "Avg Price Forecast", so fix it.)

"What are MAE, RMSE, and R²?"

MAE: the average absolute error, in the target's own units. RMSE: errors are squared, so large errors are punished more. R² = 1 − SS_res/SS_tot, the share of variance explained. 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than predicting the mean, and it can go negative (as it did for your first price target).

"Why did augmentation make CIFAR worse (74.5%)?"

Augmentation makes training harder, so it needs more epochs to converge. You stopped at 15. It regularises, so the gain shows up with longer training or a bigger model. Rotation and zoom can also hurt tiny 32×32 images. Try flip and crop only, and train 50+ epochs.

"What do BatchNorm and Dropout do?"

BatchNorm normalises each layer's activations per mini-batch, then learns a scale and shift. Training becomes faster and more stable, and it allows higher learning rates. Dropout zeroes a random fraction of units during training (25% after the conv blocks, 50% in the dense layer), so the network can't rely on any single neuron. It is off at inference.

"Why does a CNN beat an ANN on images (68–82% vs 43%)?"

A CNN uses local receptive fields and weight sharing, so it detects edges and textures anywhere in the image (translation invariance) with far fewer parameters. An ANN flattens the image and loses the spatial structure.

"Why is 'cat' the weakest class?"

"Why do LSTMs have more parameters than GRUs?"

"Why Bidirectional LSTM?"

A lip shape is ambiguous on its own (p, b and m look identical, which is the viseme problem). Context from both the past and the future frames helps decide the character.

"Explain CTC loss."

You have 75 per-frame outputs but a transcript of, say, 24 characters, with no frame-level alignment. CTC adds a blank token and sums the probability of every alignment that collapses to the target: merge repeated characters, then remove blanks. "HH-EE-LL-LL-OO" becomes "HELLO" (the blank between the two Ls keeps both). Loss = −log P(target | input). Your code wraps tf.keras.backend.ctc_batch_cost, with input_length = 75 and label_length = the non-zero label count.

"What was your evaluation set? How did you compute WER?"

The hardest question on this project. Answer honestly: "I didn't hold out a separate test split. All 1000 videos of speaker s1 were used for training, and I evaluated on a few samples. The WER and CER came from a Levenshtein-distance function I wrote, run on a small number of examples. So treat the number as a training-set indicator, not real generalisation."

The right setup: hold out about 10% of s1, or better, test on unseen speakers (s2 or others) with the full decode and WER over the whole test set. Re-run it before the interview if you can.

"Greedy vs beam search decoding?"

Greedy takes the argmax per frame, then collapses. Beam search keeps the top-k partial sequences (you used width 50) and finds a higher-probability full output. Adding a language model or the GRID grammar to the beam improves it further. Watch out: your beam-search cell redefines the vocabulary in lowercase with extra characters, so it doesn't match training. It also hard-codes word replacements ('gaon' → 'now'). Fix both before anyone reads the notebook.

"Explain your ingestion pipeline."

PyPDFDirectoryLoader reads data/policies. RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter(chunk_size=500, chunk_overlap=50) splits on paragraph, then sentence, then word. HuggingFaceEmbeddings("all-MiniLM-L6-v2") embeds the chunks, and Chroma.from_documents(persist_directory="data/chroma_db") stores them. The folder is wiped before each rebuild, so re-running doesn't add duplicates. The result is 8 chunks. Note: the 3 .md policy files in that folder are not indexed, because the loader reads only PDFs.

"Why chunk size 500 with 50 overlap?"

About 100 words fits one policy rule plus its context. MiniLM's input limit is 256 tokens, and 500 characters is about 125 tokens, so the chunk is never truncated. The 50-character overlap keeps a sentence from being cut between chunks. With only 8 chunks the corpus is tiny. Real hospital documents would need tuning against an eval set.

"Why k = 3?"

It is enough context to cover a rule that spans two chunks, without stuffing in irrelevant text, and it suits a small model's limited context. With 8 chunks total, 3 is already 37% of the corpus. At scale you'd retrieve 20, rerank, and keep 3–5.

FastAPI resolves the dependency chain before the route body runs: get_db provides a session (closed in finally), the token is read from the header, decoded, the user is loaded, and the role is checked. Auth is written once and reused across 10+ routes.

"Why look the user up in the DB when the role is already in the token?"

A role change or a deleted user takes effect immediately, because the DB is the source of truth. The cost is one DB query per request. Trusting the token's role claim alone is faster, but stale until the token expires.

"What does get_db with yield do?"

It is a generator dependency. FastAPI runs the code before yield (open a session), injects the session, and after the response runs finally (close the session), even on errors. That gives one session per request.

"Security review of your own project: what would you fix?"

Expect this in a panel. List the issues before they find them:

  1. Anyone can register as admin: /register accepts role from the request body. Fix: always set "student" there, and create admins through a seed script or an admin-only route.
  2. Tokens never expire: create_token sets no exp. Fix: add "exp": datetime.utcnow() + timedelta(minutes=30); jose then rejects expired tokens.
  3. Hard-coded secret ("supersecretkey"). Fix: load it from an environment variable or a .env file, and make it long and random.
  4. Token in localStorage, so XSS can steal it. Fix: an httpOnly cookie.

Server-side means one consistent calculation that the client can't tamper with. The current code fetches all results and averages them in Python. SQL can do it in the database:

from sqlalchemy import func
avg = db.query(func.avg(models.Result.marks)).filter(models.Result.student_id == uid).scalar()

That is better at scale, because it moves less data.

"What's an ORM session? What do commit and autoflush do?"

A session is a unit of work that tracks the objects you add or change. commit() writes them to the DB in one transaction. With autoflush=False, pending changes aren't sent before each query. check_same_thread=False is needed because FastAPI may use a session on a different thread than the one that created the SQLite connection.

"Why SQLite? When would you switch?"

It needs zero setup, is a single file, and is fine for a demo. Switch to PostgreSQL when there are many concurrent writers (SQLite locks the whole DB on write) or for production. With SQLAlchemy, that only needs a change to DATABASE_URL.

"How did you ensemble them?"

Most likely by averaging predicted probabilities (soft voting), possibly weighted. Know which method you used. Averaging works because the two models make partly different errors, which reduces variance.

"Where was SMOTE applied?"

Only on the training fold, after the split, ideally inside the CV loop (imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline). If you applied it before the split, say so and explain why that leaks.

"How does SMOTE work?"

For each minority sample, pick one of its k nearest minority neighbours and create a synthetic point at a random spot on the line between them. It balances the classes without exact duplicates. The risk is creating noise when classes overlap.

"Threshold tuning: how, and on what data?"

Predict probabilities on the validation set, sweep the threshold (e.g. 0.05–0.95), and pick the one with the best Macro F1 (for multi-class, possibly per class). Apply that fixed threshold to the test set. Never tune on the test set.

"Why Macro F1 and not accuracy or weighted F1?"

Anomalies are rare. Accuracy and weighted F1 are dominated by the normal class. Macro F1 averages per-class F1 equally, so the rare class counts as much as the common one.
